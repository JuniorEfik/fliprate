## Closest benchmark and method families

### Sycophancy and answer-changing evaluations

**Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations** (Perez et al., 2022) reports model-written evaluations and observes that larger models repeat a dialogue user's preferred answer, labelled sycophancy. This is foundational for evaluation-based detection but is broader than controlled evidence-versus-pressure updating.

**Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models** (Sharma et al., 2023; updated 2025) studies sycophancy across free-form tasks and links it partly to human and preference-model preferences for responses matching user views. It motivates pressure-resistance evaluation but does not, from the abstract alone, establish the matched factorial design proposed for FlipRate.

**SycEval: Evaluating LLM Sycophancy** (Fanous et al., 2025) evaluates multiple models across mathematics and medical tasks. Its abstract distinguishes progressive changes that lead to correct answers from regressive changes that lead to incorrect answers, and varies rebuttal timing and style. This substantially overlaps FlipRate's initial framing.

**Are You Sure You're Sure? Two Confounds in a Sycophancy Benchmark** (Gupta et al., 2026) audits SycEval and reports that target-answer naming and output-format placement are confounded with the intended conditions, reversing some comparisons after control. This is directly actionable: FlipRate must equalise target-answer disclosure and grading instructions across conditions.

**Sycophancy Suppression Can Impair Rational Updating** (Ma et al., 2026, EMNLP Findings per arXiv comment) explicitly separates Unsupported-Yielding from Rational-Updating in a two-turn framework and studies intervention trade-offs. This is the closest conceptual competitor and invalidates any claim that FlipRate first distinguishes good updating from bad yielding.

### Belief revision and social pressure

**MEDLEY-BENCH** (Abtahi et al., 2026) compares private self-review with analyst-conditioned social revision, includes manipulated consensus labels, and evaluates 35 models. Its abstract explicitly frames behavioural metacognition and belief revision under social pressure. The v1.0 abstract also discloses scoring and pipeline limitations and announces a corrected v1.5 rerun.

**Beyond Social Pressure: Benchmarking Epistemic Attack in Large Language Models** (Au and Noronha, 2026) introduces PPT-Bench with baseline, single-turn pressure, and multi-turn Socratic escalation across four pressure types. It already covers multi-turn conversational capitulation, so FlipRate must distinguish its evidence controls and scoring rather than claim multi-turn pressure as new.

### Honesty, manipulation, and broader behavioural evaluation

**The MASK Benchmark** (Ren et al., 2025; updated 2026) aims to disentangle honesty from accuracy and measures lying under pressure. It is adjacent rather than identical: MASK focuses on honesty/lying, while FlipRate observes answer and confidence revision under rebuttal. Its distinction between correctness and honesty reinforces the need to avoid inferring internal beliefs from text alone.

**DarkBench** (Kran et al., 2025, ICLR 2025 Oral per arXiv comment) evaluates dark patterns across six categories, including sycophancy. It establishes a broader manipulation and product-design context but is not, from the abstract, a targeted evidence-versus-pressure belief-updating protocol.

### Confidence and evidence updating

**ForecastBench** (Karger et al., 2024; updated 2025) is a dynamic forecasting-accuracy benchmark using future-event questions and human comparisons. It motivates dynamic, leakage-resistant questions and calibrated probabilities but does not itself evaluate rebuttal pressure.

**Do Language Models Update their Forecasts with New Information?** (Yuan et al., 2025; updated 2026) introduces EvolveCast and reports that models often update in the correct direction but under-react quantitatively, with poorly calibrated verbal and logit confidence. This is directly relevant to FlipRate's confidence-drift and evidence-responsiveness components.

## Preliminary synthesis

**Observed:** the literature already contains all of the following components separately or in partial combination: sycophantic answer changes, progressive versus regressive changes, rational updating versus unsupported yielding, social-pressure belief revision, multi-turn escalation, confidence updating, and benchmarks separating related behavioural constructs.

**Interpreted:** FlipRate's viable gap is not a new construct label. It may lie in combining matched evidence and pressure manipulations with multi-turn trajectories, rigorous prompt-template controls, initial-correctness-conditioned metrics, and auditable raw records.

**Limitation:** this conclusion is abstract-grounded. Full texts must be read before any claim about what prior protocols do not contain.

## Immediate design lessons from prior work

1. Keep target-answer naming identical across compared conditions.
2. Keep output-format and grading instructions in the same location across conditions.
3. Separate evidence addition from rhetorical intensity.
4. Condition metrics on initial correctness and evidence direction.
5. Include a no-rebuttal interaction-length control.
6. Preserve confidence as a separate outcome and treat calibration limitations explicitly.
7. Do not call all answer changes sycophancy.
8. Do not call all stability epistemically good.
9. Preserve prompt versions and full conversations for confound audits.
